The Fifth Idea

Final year. We walked in with five project ideas — and four of them quietly fell away.

The fifth was the one that stuck: teach a machine to read the retina.

The panel said yes. Three long weeks later, we can finally say it — we made it.

2
3

Why the Eye

The retina is the one place in the body where you can watch blood vessels and nerves directly — no scalpel, just light.

Hidden in one fundus photo are the early warnings of diseases that steal sight in silence. But reading them takes a specialist, and specialists are rare where screening is needed most.

So we asked a stubborn question: could a single photo be screened for several diseases at once?

Three Thieves of Sight

We chose three diseases that quietly take vision.

Retinopathy of Prematurity, which threatens babies born too soon. Diabetic Retinopathy, the toll diabetes exacts on the eye. And Glaucoma — the silent one, unnoticed until sight is already gone.

Three very different patients, one shared goal: catch it early.

The Hunt for Data

A model is only as good as what it learns from — so first we went hunting for eyes.

For diabetic retinopathy we pooled EyePACS and APTOS: nearly 39,000 graded photos. For glaucoma, SMDG-19 and G1020 — about 13,000 more.

ROP had no clean public set. So we built our own: 5,004 infant retinas from 188 babies, published on Kaggle ourselves.

8

One shared pipeline

→

Every image cropped tight to the retina, then contrast-balanced — so the model sees the eye, not the camera.

9

Cleaning, and Playing Fair

Raw fundus photos are messy — black borders, uneven light, every camera different. So every image took the same path: cropped tight to the retina, then evened out with CLAHE.

And we refused to cheat. For the babies we split by child, so no infant's eyes appeared in both training and testing.

A model that has already seen the answer isn't learning — it's memorising.

10

We raced five architectures

AUC 0.96

ResNet-50 won the short sweep. At full training the gap nearly vanished (0.927 vs 0.920) and a significance test said the difference could be chance — so we kept it, but stopped claiming it was the better architecture.

11

Building the Brains

We didn't guess at the models. For ROP we raced five architectures head-to-head; ResNet-50 won, so ResNet-50 it was. Diabetic retinopathy and glaucoma run on EfficientNetV2-S.

Each one studies a 384-pixel image five times over — flipped and rotated, then averaged — so one odd angle never decides a diagnosis.

13

Trained on Borrowed Machines

We trained all of this without owning a single GPU. Everything ran on Kaggle's free P100s and T4s — sessions that died after twelve hours, so we saved progress every epoch and resumed the next day.

One week vanished into a single cryptic CUDA error, until we pinned the exact PyTorch version. After that the runs settled: about four hours for DR, ninety minutes for ROP, two for glaucoma.

15

The Near-Failure

Our favourite lesson came from almost failing.

The glaucoma model scored a brilliant 0.97 at home — then we showed it a hospital it had never seen, and it crumbled to 0.59.

That gap is the real world. We didn't bury it; we folded the new source into training and watched it climb back to 0.96. That's when a model becomes trustworthy.

What It Does Today

Today the product is focused: ROP only. Upload one neonatal fundus photo and it answers in seconds — a heatmap of where it looked, a graded risk, a one-click PDF report, and a 6-class ICROP staging research preview.

ROP screening ICROP staging · preview

The DR and glaucoma models we built along the way were retired from the product — our own audits found their headline numbers confounded, and this demo only exposes what we currently stand behind. It is not a doctor, and we never pretend it is.
